# Literature counterexample review: 037 "What our own errors say about the limits of the current method"

- **Раздел/строка CLAIMS:** articles/037, Claim 2 (§2, lines 73–93), Claim 3 (§3, lines 97–124), P4 (§4, lines 177–190), terminology (§4, P1–P4, lines 135–204)
- **Вердикт:** держится с оговоркой
- **Что пересчитано:** не числа. Проверены первичные источники по каждому кандидату в контрпример: arXiv:2406.19108 (Computational Life), arXiv:2607.01483 (BFF, follow-up), arXiv:2402.03961 (Evoloops 25 years), arXiv:1607.01750 / Sci Rep 7:997 (Adams et al.), Nature 423:139 (Lenski et al. 2003), Ray 1991 (Tierra), Gruber 1993 (ontology), Seltman ch.14 / Loftus & Masson (within-subject). Полный список — §Sources.
- **Расхождение:** Claim 2 stated in the strong universal form ("Every design… Remove the condition and the behaviour disappears, exactly and completely") is contradicted in the literature: at least two published results show behaviour that was not a consequence of a programmed condition, and one published result shows that a claimed emergence was an artefact of the measurement. Claim 3 as written is not falsifiable and cannot be supported by the evidence the manifesto cites. P4 is much stronger than the manifesto realises: it is essentially a theorem, which is good for the manifesto and bad for its framing as a "principle".
- **Проверки на устойчивость:** Each counterexample was checked against the strong and the weak reading of the claim. The strong reading ("no behaviour can exceed what was programmed") fails; the weak reading ("the class of implemented designs has not produced a new ontology") survives. Both readings are reported below, because the manifesto needs to choose one.
- **Что убьёт утверждение:** For Claim 2, a published system where a behaviour appears, is removed by deleting a condition, and *also* appears in a control where that condition was never programmed. For Claim 3, a single published case where incremental extension produced a new ontology, or a demonstration that the claim makes no prediction. For P4, a closed deterministic system whose future is not a function of its recorded state — which would contradict the Church–Turing/determinism assumptions, not merely a design choice.
- **Что предложить ведущему:** Downgrade Claim 2 from a universal claim about *all* designs to a claim about *the designs we implemented*, and cite the two papers that break the universal form. Delete or reformulate Claim 3, which is currently unfalsifiable. Keep P4 but state it as a theorem with its assumptions, not as a principle — it is the one part of the manifesto that is genuinely deductive, and it is being undersold.

## Verdict in one paragraph

The manifesto is right about its own failures and wrong about the size of the conclusion it draws from them. Claim 2 in its strong form — that every living-looking behaviour in the implementable literature is a consequence of a programmed condition, and that removing the condition removes the behaviour "exactly and completely" — is **false as a universal claim**: two published results (Computational Life 2024, and Evoloops/Sayama 1999 as reviewed in 2024) show behaviour emerging from conditions that were *not* specified, and a third (BFF 2026) shows that the most celebrated of these emergences does not require the mechanism that was claimed to produce it. Claim 3 is **not falsifiable as written** and should be withdrawn or restated; the literature it invokes does not support "cannot reach a different ontology", only "has not yet reached one". P4 is the strongest part of the document and the manifesto **understates** it: it is close to a theorem (Poincaré recurrence + determinism), and presenting it as a "principle" invites an attack that a theorem would not. The terminology check finds real collisions: "two-way coupling", "within-subject", and "ontology" all have established meanings that differ from the manifesto's, and using them without definition will read as error rather than as novelty.

## CLAIM 2

> "Every design in the current literature that we could implement … is a machine whose behaviour is a function of conditions that were programmed in advance. … Remove the condition and the behaviour disappears."

### Counterexample 2.1 — Computational Life: self-replicators from random programs

**Citation.** Agüera y Arcas, B., Alakuijala, J., Evans, J., Laurie, B., Mordvintsev, A., Niklasson, E., Randazzo, E., & Versari, L. (2024). *Computational Life: How Well-formed, Self-replicating Programs Emerge from Simple Interaction.* arXiv:2406.19108. DOI: [10.48550/arXiv.2406.19108](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2406.19108).

**What it shows.** Random, non-self-replicating programs in a soup with *no explicit fitness landscape* spontaneously produce self-replicators, via random interactions and self-modification, with and without background random mutation. The authors' own abstract states the essential point for this review: the soup contains **no fitness function, no target, and no replication instruction**. The condition that produces the behaviour is *the absence of a condition* — random interaction in a Turing-complete substrate. There is no term in the program that says "replicate"; replication is a property of the trajectory, discovered by the system.

**Does it break or qualify?** It **breaks the strong form** of Claim 2 and **qualifies the weak form**.

- Breaks: the manifesto says the behaviour is "a consequence of those conditions" that were "programmed in advance". Here the self-replication is a consequence of *randomness plus a universal substrate*, not of any condition the authors chose. Deleting "the condition" is not possible, because the condition is "random interaction in a Turing-complete language" — the weakest possible condition, which is the manifesto's own ideal (P1: fewer knobs is more rigorous). The paper satisfies the manifesto's own criterion for rigour and still produces the living-looking behaviour.
- Qualifies: one can still argue that "random interaction in a Turing-complete substrate" *is* a programmed condition, and that the manifesto's claim survives in the trivial sense that everything in a program is programmed. But this is exactly the reading under which Claim 2 becomes unfalsifiable, and the manifesto must not have it both ways.

**What the manifesto must change.** Either restrict Claim 2 to "the designs we implemented" (which is defensible and is what §2's own evidence actually supports), or add an explicit argument for why "a Turing-complete substrate with random interaction" counts as a programmed condition while "a resource field with renewal" does not. The current text does not make that argument, and a referee will ask for it.

### Counterexample 2.2 — The BFF follow-up: the emergence was real, but the claimed mechanism was not necessary

**Citation.** Knierim, C., Versari, L., Obryk, R., Agüera y Arcas, B., & Saurous, R. A. (2026). *BFF: Simple explanations for complex phenomena.* arXiv:2607.01483. DOI: [10.48550/arXiv.2607.01483](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2607.01483).

**What it shows.** Using a direct self-replicator detector (rather than the "takeover" proxy used in 2024), the authors show that:

1. The average time to the **first** self-replicator in BFF is ≈2.5·10⁶ interactions (5·10⁶ programs); plain uniform random sampling of 64-byte strings takes ≈2.9·10⁷ programs — only ~6× slower.
2. Sampling from a hand-chosen distribution (CUST64) finds a self-replicator in 9.4·10⁴ programs — **~25× faster than running the BFF system itself**.
3. Blocking mergers (the compositional mechanism the 2024 paper implicitly credited) does **not** prevent self-replicators from appearing — it only prevents them from taking over the soup. "Compositionality is not needed for the discovery of self-replicators in the BFF system."

**Does it break or qualify?** It **qualifies Counterexample 2.1 and simultaneously breaks Claim 2 in a different way.** This is the most interesting result in the set, because it cuts both ways:

- Against the manifesto: the self-replicators *do* appear without the mechanism that was supposed to produce them. Behaviour appeared that was not a consequence of the programmed condition (pairwise interaction / compositionality). That is literally the manifesto's falsifier: "Remove the condition and the behaviour disappears" — here the condition was removed and the behaviour did not disappear.
- Against the 2024 paper: the "emergence" was partly an artefact of the *measurement* (gelation/takeover as a proxy for appearance). This is the manifesto's Claim 1 error shape appearing in someone else's paper — the design measured a consequence without establishing that the mechanism could act. The manifesto should cite this as **corroboration of Claim 1**, which strengthens its credibility.

**What the manifesto must change.** This is the single most useful citation the manifesto is missing. It supports Claim 1 (the error shape is general, not the authors' private failing) and it forces Claim 2 into its defensible weak form. Citing it makes the manifesto look better read, not worse.

### Counterexample 2.3 — Evoloops: Darwinian evolution from no designed ancestor

**Citation.** Sayama, H. (1999). *A new structurally dissolvable self-reproducing loop evolving in a simple cellular automata space.* Artificial Life, 5(4), 343–365. Review: Sayama, H., & Nehaniv, C. L. (2024). *Self-Reproduction and Evolution in Cellular Automata: 25 Years after Evoloops.* arXiv:2402.03961; Artificial Life 31(1), 81–104. DOI: [10.1162/artl_a_00451](https://doi.org/10.1162/artl_a_00451).

**What it shows.** Evoloops were the first cellular-automata artificial life system to exhibit Darwinian evolution of self-reproducing organisms by *spontaneous variation and natural selection* in a deterministic nine-state five-neighbour CA. The key design move was **structural dissolution**: loops dissolve when they cannot acquire resources, which frees the space for descendants. There is no ancestor placed in the world, no fitness function, and no programmed variation — variation arises from collisions and from the dissolution rule.

**Does it break or qualify?** It **qualifies** rather than breaks. The manifesto anticipated this: it says "The models that have produced open-ended behaviour all did so by adding a mechanism to an existing ontology: a new instruction, a new resource, a new interaction" (§3). Evoloops is exactly that — structural dissolution is a new interaction rule. So this is **not a counterexample to Claim 2**, and the manifesto is right to have pre-empted it.

However, the 2024 review is a **counterexample to the manifesto's claim that the literature is explicit that the boundary is "the class of models we build cannot generate a new class"** (§3, lines 110–111). The review's §4 is a list of open problems — autopoiesis, self-examination reproduction, universality, avoiding pseudo-equilibrium — and its authors treat these as *engineering challenges*, not as a proven boundary of the class. The manifesto attributes to the literature a conclusion the literature does not draw. This is a citation error, not a scientific one, but a referee will catch it.

### Counterexample 2.4 — Avida: complex features with no programmed target

**Citation.** Lenski, R. E., Ofria, C., Pennock, R. T., & Adami, C. (2003). *The evolutionary origin of complex features.* Nature, 423, 139–144. DOI: [10.1038/nature01568](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature01568).

**What it shows.** Digital organisms evolved the ability to perform complex logic functions (including EQU, the most complex) from simpler ones. The functions are *rewarded* if performed, but the organisms are not programmed to perform them; the sequence of intermediate functions and the route to EQU were not specified.

**Does it break or qualify?** It **qualifies, and in a way that helps the manifesto.** The reward structure ("perform EQU and get CPU time") is a programmed condition, and removing it removes the behaviour — so the manifesto's claim survives. But the *specific* complex function that evolved, and the path to it, were not programmed. This is a precise example of the distinction the manifesto needs: **the condition selects, but does not specify.** Claim 2 conflates "the behaviour is a function of a programmed condition" with "the behaviour was specified by a programmed condition". These are different, and only the second is false here.

**What the manifesto must change.** Add the select/specify distinction explicitly. It is the difference between a claim that is true and interesting ("we programmed the selection, not the outcome") and one that is false ("we programmed the outcome").

### Counterexample 2.5 — Tierra: an ecology that was not programmed

**Citation.** Ray, T. S. (1991). *An approach to the synthesis of life.* In Artificial Life II (pp. 371–408). Addison-Wesley. Author's page: [tomray.me/pubs/tierra](https://tomray.me/pubs/tierra).

**What it shows.** From a single ancestral replicator, Tierra produced tens of thousands of self-replicating genotypes, including **parasites and hyper-parasites** — ecological roles that were not in the ancestor and were not programmed. The "host-parasite" relation is an emergent property of the instruction set, not a term in the code.

**Does it break or qualify?** It **qualifies.** The instruction set is a programmed condition, and the ecology disappears if the instruction set is changed — so the manifesto's strong claim survives here. But the *roles* were not programmed, only the substrate. Same distinction as 2.4: selection/substrate vs. specification.

**Honest note.** I could not verify from the primary source that any Tierra result shows a *new level of organisation* (a new kind of object) rather than a new *role* within the existing ontology. The literature I found treats Tierra's parasites as new ecological roles, not as a new ontology. **Not found:** a Tierra result that supports Claim 3.

### Verdict on Claim 2

| Counterexample | Breaks | Qualifies |
|---|---|---|
| 2.1 Computational Life (2024) | **Yes** — strong form | Yes — weak form survives |
| 2.2 BFF (2026) | **Yes** — condition removed, behaviour remained | Yes — and corroborates Claim 1 |
| 2.3 Evoloops | No | Yes — pre-empted by §3; but §3 mis-cites the review |
| 2.4 Avida | No | Yes — forces select/specify distinction |
| 2.5 Tierra | No | Yes — same distinction |

**Claim 2 survives only in the weak form.** The strong form ("every design… remove the condition and the behaviour disappears, exactly and completely") is broken by 2.1 and 2.2.

## CLAIM 3

> "The scientific graph grows from below by incremental extension, and it cannot reach a different ontology."

### Finding 3.1 — The claim is not falsifiable as written

**What this is.** This is not a citation but a logical finding, and it is the most important one in this section. The claim has the form "the graph cannot reach X". To test it, one needs a case where the graph *did* reach a different ontology. But the manifesto defines "a different ontology" only negatively — "not a better parameter, but a different kind of object" (§3, line 105). There is no operational test for "different kind of object". Consequently:

- If a case is found where a new kind of object appeared, the manifesto can say "that was an extension of the same ontology" (as it does for Evoloops in §3).
- If no case is found, the manifesto can say "as predicted".

This is the structure the manifesto itself condemns in §5: "A manifesto that cannot fail is a slogan." **Claim 3 is currently a slogan by the manifesto's own standard.** This is the single most damaging internal inconsistency in the document, and it does not require any literature to establish.

### Finding 3.2 — The literature does not support the claim the manifesto attributes to it

**Citation.** Sayama & Nehaniv (2024), as above. The review's §4 ("Future Challenges and Open Problems") lists autopoiesis, self-examination-based reproduction, universality, and escape from pseudo-equilibrium as **open problems**, and explicitly says "it remains unclear how one could integrate autopoietic dynamics into existing cellular automata models" — *unclear*, not *impossible*. The manifesto (§3, lines 110–111) writes: "The literature we surveyed is explicit about this: the boundary is not 'we have not found the right parameters' but 'the class of models we build cannot generate a new class.'" **The literature is not explicit about this.** It is explicit about the opposite: that these are unsolved problems.

**Does it break or qualify?** It **breaks the evidential basis** of Claim 3, though not the claim itself.

### Finding 3.3 — A candidate counterexample, and why it does not qualify

**Citation.** Adams, A., Zenil, H., Davies, P. C. W., & Walker, S. I. (2017). *Formal Definitions of Unbounded Evolution and Innovation Reveal Universal Mechanisms for Open-Ended Evolution in Dynamical Systems.* Scientific Reports, 7, 997. DOI: [10.1038/s41598-017-00810-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-00810-8).

**What it shows.** The authors formalise "unbounded evolution" (non-repeating within the Poincaré recurrence time of an equivalent isolated system) and "innovation" (trajectories not observed in isolated systems), and show that **state-dependent, self-referential, open** cellular automata satisfy both criteria, while externally driven and isolated systems do not. This is the closest thing in the literature to a formal demonstration that a system can generate trajectories that no fixed-rule system of the same size can produce.

**Does it break or qualify?** It **qualifies Claim 3 and strongly supports P4.** It does not produce a new ontology — the objects are still CA cells and rules. But it shows that *novelty in the formal sense* is achievable, and it identifies the mechanism: self-reference coupled to openness. That is P4, formalised, in a peer-reviewed journal. The manifesto should cite it under P4, not under Claim 3.

**Honest note on Claim 3.** I looked for a published case where incremental extension produced a new ontology and **did not find one**. The philosophy-of-science literature on incommensurability (Kuhn; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "The Incommensurability of Scientific Theories", [plato.stanford.edu](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/incommensurability/)) describes ontology change in science, but it describes it as a *historical phenomenon*, not as something a computational model has produced. **Not found** is the honest answer here, and it is worth stating plainly: the absence of a counterexample is weak evidence, because the claim is also unfalsifiable.

### Verdict on Claim 3

**Claim 3 does not hold as stated.** It is unfalsifiable, it mis-cites the literature, and no counterexample was found — which, given the unfalsifiability, is not evidence for it. The defensible version is much weaker: *"The designs we surveyed have not produced a new ontology; we do not know whether they can."* That is a statement of ignorance, not a boundary theorem, and the manifesto should say so.

## CLAIM P4

> "Novelty cannot originate inside an agent whose state is a record; it must enter from the environment, another agent, or a source that is not a function of the record."

### Finding P4.1 — This is (nearly) a theorem, and the manifesto should say so

**Citation.** Adams et al. (2017), as above; and the Poincaré recurrence theorem as used there.

**What it shows.** Adams et al. state the result the manifesto is groping for: for a finite, closed deterministic system, the Poincaré recurrence time is "an absolute upper-bound on when such a system will terminate any appearance of open-endedness". They further note that their definitions "automatically exclude finite deterministic systems unless they are open to external perturbations in some way", and that "unbounded evolution … is only possible for subsystem interacting with an external environment". Their Case I (self-referential *and* open) is the only variant that scales.

This is P4, with a proof sketch and a citation. It is stronger than the manifesto's version, because it is a statement about *all* finite closed deterministic systems, not about "an agent whose state is a record". The manifesto is **underselling its own best argument** by calling it a "principle" and a "deduction … from what a record is" (§4, line 184), when in fact it follows from determinism plus finiteness.

**Does it break or qualify?** It **strengthens P4** and forces a correction to its framing.

### Finding P4.2 — The manifesto's statement of P4 is ambiguous in a way that matters

**What this is.** "Novelty cannot originate inside an agent whose state is a record" is ambiguous between:

- **(a) Trivial reading:** the agent computes only functions of its record, so its output is a function of its record. True by definition, and uninteresting — it excludes nothing, because every deterministic program is a function of its state.
- **(b) Substantive reading:** no closed system can produce a trajectory that is not eventually periodic / not bounded by its recurrence time. This is the Poincaré result, and it is true and interesting.

The manifesto's "what would falsify it" (§4, lines 188–190) tests reading (b) — "a cell whose entire state is a record, in a world with no external source of novelty, that nevertheless produces behaviour not predictable from its record". But the *principle* is stated in reading (a). Under (a) the falsifier is impossible by definition (which the manifesto admits: "We do not believe this is possible"), and under (b) the falsifier would require violating determinism. **In neither reading can the principle fail**, which means it is not a falsifiable principle — it is a definition plus a theorem, and the manifesto should present it as such.

**Does it break or qualify?** It **qualifies** — and the repair is easy and makes the document stronger.

### Finding P4.3 — No counterexample found

I searched for a closed deterministic system producing genuine novelty and **found none**, as expected. The literature on computational closure (Rosen), no-free-lunch (Wolpert & Macready), and algorithmic information theory is consistent with P4 rather than in tension with it. **Not found** is the correct answer, and it is the right answer here.

### Verdict on P4

**P4 holds**, but not as a "principle" that could be falsified by an experiment. It is a theorem with assumptions (finiteness, determinism, closedness), and presenting it as a principle invites the objection that it is a tautology. The fix: state the assumptions, cite Adams et al. (2017) and the recurrence result, and drop the "what would falsify it" paragraph — replacing it with "what would violate its assumptions" (an open system, a stochastic element, or an infinite state space).

## TERMINOLOGY CHECK — terms already taken with a different meaning

This section is a genuine risk to the document's reception. Each of the four terms below has an established meaning in the literature; using them in the manifesto's sense without definition will read as error to a referee.

### "Two-way coupling" — **collides**

**Established meaning.** In fluid–structure interaction (FSI) and multiphysics, "one-way coupling" means the fluid acts on the structure but the structure's deformation does not feed back; "two-way coupling" means the modification of the structure has a non-negligible influence on the flow. See e.g. *A Comparison of One-Way and Two-Way Coupling Methods for Numerical Analysis of Fluid-Structure Interactions*, J. Applied Mathematics (2011), DOI: [10.1155/2011/853560](https://doi.org/10.1155/2011/853560).

**Collision.** The manifesto's P2 uses "two-way coupling" to mean *the agents write the same state that sets their own terms* — a **self-referential closure** property, not a bidirectional physical coupling. These are different: FSI two-way coupling is between two *distinct* media, and neither is required to set its own parameters. A referee from engineering will read P2 as a statement about FSI and find it confused.

**Recommendation.** Use a different term: "self-referential closure", "endogenous terms", or "the agents write the state that sets their terms". Cite the FSI distinction explicitly to show the collision is understood.

### "Within-subject measure" — **collides**

**Established meaning.** In experimental design and psychometrics, a within-subjects (repeated-measures) design is one where each subject experiences all levels of the independent variable; the measure is *within-subject* because the comparison is made within a subject across conditions. See Seltman, *Experimental Design and Analysis*, ch. 14 ([stat.cmu.edu](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~hseltman/309/Book/chapter14.pdf)); Loftus & Masson (1994) and its generalisation (Psychon. Bull. Rev., DOI: [10.3758/s13423-012-0230-1](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-012-0230-1)).

**Collision.** The manifesto's P3 uses "within-cell measure" to mean *the fraction of a cell's behavioural variance explained by its current situation versus its own history* — a **variance decomposition within one cell's trajectory over time**, not a repeated-measures design across conditions. These are related but not the same: a within-subject design compares conditions, whereas the manifesto's measure decomposes variance between two predictors (situation vs. history) for a single cell.

**Recommendation.** Define it explicitly on first use, and prefer "within-trajectory variance decomposition" or "situation-vs-history decomposition". The manifesto's P3 argument (that a between-cell measure can be moved by a bonus while a within-cell measure cannot) is actually a good argument, but it is not the standard within-subject argument, and stating it in standard language will make it look like a mistake.

### "Ontology" (in the modelling sense) — **collides, and the collision is substantive**

**Established meaning.** In computer science, an ontology is "a formal specification of a conceptualization" (Gruber, 1993) — a set of representational primitives (classes, attributes, relations) with which to model a domain. See Gruber, *What is an Ontology?* ([tomgruber.org](https://tomgruber.org/writing/definition-of-ontology.pdf)); and for the modelling-and-simulation use, *Ontology for Modeling and Simulation*, WSC 2010 ([informs-sim.org](https://informs-sim.org/wsc10papers/058.pdf)).

**Collision.** The manifesto uses "ontology" in a **philosophical** sense — "a different kind of object", a different set of *what exists* — while the modelling literature uses it in a **representational** sense — a different set of *primitives for representing*. These are different claims: "the model cannot represent a new kind of object" (representational) is a claim about expressiveness and is testable (a Turing-complete substrate can represent anything); "the world cannot contain a new kind of object" (philosophical) is a claim about reality and is not testable in a simulation at all.

**Recommendation.** This is the most important terminology fix, because the collision is what makes Claim 3 unfalsifiable. If the manifesto means the representational sense, Claim 3 is **false** for any Turing-complete substrate (Computational Life is the counterexample). If it means the philosophical sense, Claim 3 is not a scientific claim. The manifesto must choose, and either choice changes the claim.

### "Constraints on flows" — **no collision found, but no established meaning either**

I searched for this phrase and **found no established technical meaning** in the literature. It reads as the manifesto's own coinage. This is *good* — no collision — but it means the term carries no authority and must be defined from scratch. The related established notions are **conservation laws** and **flux constraints** in continuum mechanics and in resource-based modelling; the manifesto should say which of these it is borrowing from, and what it adds.

### "Novelty from outside" — **no collision found**

No established technical meaning located. The nearest established notions are **exogenous variation** (economics, statistics), **external perturbation** (dynamical systems), and **innovation** as defined by Adams et al. (2017) — "trajectories not observed in isolated systems". The last is the closest and the manifesto should probably adopt it, since it is formal and peer-reviewed.

## The strongest single counterexample

**Agüera y Arcas et al. (2024), *Computational Life*, arXiv:2406.19108 — together with its 2026 follow-up, Knierim et al., arXiv:2607.01483.**

Reason: it is the only case in the literature that attacks Claim 2 at its strongest point. The manifesto's argument is that any implementable design has the behaviour *in* it, because the behaviour is a consequence of a condition the modeller chose. Computational Life has **no fitness function, no replication instruction, and no target** — the weakest possible condition, exactly the "fewer knobs" ideal the manifesto sets for itself in P1 — and self-replication appears anyway. The 2026 follow-up then shows that even the *mechanism* the 2024 paper credited (pairwise interaction and compositionality) is not necessary: removing it slows the emergence but does not prevent it. So the counterexample is robust in both directions — it survives the "but the substrate is programmed" objection (the substrate is the *minimum* condition, not a chosen one) and it survives the "but the mechanism was programmed" objection (the mechanism was removed and the behaviour remained).

The manifesto cannot answer this with a parameter argument, because there is no parameter. It can only answer by restricting Claim 2 to the designs it actually implemented — which is what it should do, and which is what §2's own evidence supports.
