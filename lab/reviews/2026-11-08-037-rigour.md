## Finding 1 — Claim 1 ("seven errors, one shape") is a post-hoc category, and four of the seven do not fit it

**What is claimed.** §1: "In every one of the seven, the design **assumed the mechanism was present** and then measured its consequences. In none of them was there a step that established, **before the measurement**, that the mechanism could act." And: "The check that would have caught all seven is one sentence long: *compare two arms that must differ; if they are bit-identical, the manipulation does not reach the dynamics.*"

**Why it fails.**

The seven rows are not one shape. They fall into at least three mutually exclusive categories, and the sentence "the check that would have caught all seven" is false for the majority:

| # | row as written | does it fit "assumed a mechanism was present"? | would the bit-identity check have caught it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "a mechanism that added resource every step" — "a tap, not a feedback loop" | **No.** A tap *is* present and *does* act. The error is that the mechanism is not the one claimed (no feedback). This is a mischaracterised mechanism, not an absent one. | No. The arms differ; the tap runs. |
| 2 | "two arms given unequal doses" | **No.** The mechanism acted — in both arms, unequally. The error is a confound in the comparison, not an inert manipulation. | **No, and this is decisive.** Unequal doses make the arms *differ more*, not less. Bit-identity is the opposite of this failure. |
| 3 | "an effect clipped by the resource ceiling" — "the recorded dose was not the dose applied" | **No.** The mechanism acted and was clipped by saturation. This is a **measurement/logging** error (the recorded dose ≠ the applied dose). | No. The arms differ. |
| 4 | "a trait the world never read" — "the 'selection' was a tautology" | **Yes**, this is the shape. | Yes. |
| 5 | "a 'paired design' that was a no-op" — "the arms were already seeded identically" | **Yes**, this is the shape. | Yes. |
| 6 | "a signal advantage that did not replicate" — "one lead, ten seeds, p = 0.28" | **No.** This is a **statistical power / selection-of-lead** error. The manipulation reached the dynamics; the effect was not distinguishable from noise. Nothing was inert. | No. The arms differed; the difference was just not significant. |
| 7 | "a zero-cost control that could not fail" — "at zero cost the trait entered no equation" | **Partly.** The *control arm* was inert, but the finding ("selection was the trait's price") is a **correct** result obtained by an inert control, not an error that survived into a report and was retracted. The row is describing a valid negative result as if it were a defect. | Yes, in the trivial sense that the control arm was constant. |

So: **four of seven (1, 2, 3, 6) do not fit the stated shape**, and **four of seven (1, 2, 3, 6) could not have been caught by the one-sentence check** the document calls sufficient. Row 6 is the clearest: a non-replicating effect on ten seeds is the *paradigm* of a manipulation that reached the dynamics and produced noise. Calling it "the mechanism was not there" is a category error.

The deeper problem is selection. §6.4 concedes "It does not claim that our seven errors are representative. They are the errors we made; we argue they have a common shape, not that they are a sample." But the shape was *chosen after the fact* from the same 45-entry register that contains a great many errors of other shapes. `docs/eliminations.md` is full of rows that fit no such pattern at all — e.g. row 56 ("the value '9 ± 1' was arithmetic of the design, not a measurement"), row 50 ("pooled five-world numbers are not to be quoted"), row 36 ("the old counter compared the drop with the previous value at 1–5 cells"). Those are unit, denominator and estimator errors, not "assumed the mechanism was present". The claim is not that seven errors share a shape; it is that seven errors *were chosen* because they share a shape. That is a narrative, not a finding.

**What would fix it.** State the taxonomy honestly: separate (a) inert manipulation (4, 5, 7), (b) mischaracterised mechanism (1), (c) confounded comparison (2), (d) measurement/logging error (3), (e) underpowered claim (6). Then say which check catches which class — the bit-identity check catches class (a) only, i.e. **three of seven, not seven of seven**. Better: take all 45 elimination rows, classify them by an explicit rule stated before classification, and report the distribution. If the modal class really is (a), the claim survives in a weaker and defensible form.

## Finding 2 — Claim 2 is a tautology, and the document itself says so

**What is claimed.** §2: "**a system whose behaviour is a function of parameters we set can only exhibit behaviour we could have set.**" Then: "If the behaviour we are looking for is defined by *not* being a function of our choices, then no arrangement of our choices can produce it."

**Why it fails.** Read the second sentence carefully. It reduces to: *if X is defined as not-F, then things that are F cannot produce X.* That is the law of non-contradiction, not a finding about biology, computation, or the world. The first sentence is the same statement with the quantifier spelled out: a function of chosen quantities attains only values in the range of that function. No experiment can bear on it. It is true of every model ever built, including the ones the manifesto proposes to build.

The document half-admits this and then does not follow through. §2 "What this does not say" says: "a model whose degrees of freedom are all set by the modeller cannot exhibit a property defined as independence from the modeller's choices." That is exactly a tautology, and it is presented as a *distinction that matters* rather than as the admission that Claim 2 is vacuous. §6.2 adds: "It claims that a class of designs cannot reach a particular question, and it gives the argument for the class, not for the literature." The "argument for the class" is the tautology.

Note also that the empirical support offered for Claim 2 is not the tautology but four failed signal designs — and those four failures are, by the document's own Claim 1, errors of *inert manipulation*, i.e. the mechanism did not act. An inert mechanism tells you nothing about whether a working mechanism could have produced the behaviour. The evidence and the claim are disconnected: the evidence is about our bugs, the claim is about the form of models.

**What would make it a finding rather than a restatement.** A finding would be a statement with content that could be false. Candidates:

1. **A quantitative claim about the size of the reachable set.** "For the class of models of type C with N parameters, the set of behaviours attainable has measure zero in the space of behaviours of interest, for a stated topology on behaviour space." That is falsifiable by exhibiting a behaviour outside the set.
2. **A claim about a *specific* modeller-independent property.** Take one property P (e.g. "the agent's response function is not representable as a finite-parameter family chosen by the experimenter") and show that a named class cannot realise P, while another named class can. That is a theorem about two classes, not about "the class".
3. **A claim about *us*.** "Every design we built had the behaviour as an explicit term, and removing the term removed the behaviour" — that is an empirical claim about our code, checkable by ablation, and it is what §2's first paragraph actually offers. It is much weaker than "can only imitate", but it is a finding.
4. **The strongest available version:** the claim is false as stated, and the counterexample is in this repository (Finding 3).

## Finding 3 — Claim 2 is contradicted by the programme's own published result (article 034)

**What is claimed.** §2: "**The evidence from our own world.** Four designs were built to make a signal pay. In every one, the effect was either absent, or equal in all arms, or smaller than the difference between two implementations of the same control." And: "The cell did not use the signal; the rule did."

**Why it fails.** `articles/034-what-a-signal-should-change.md` reports a signal that pays, in the same world, on 20 seeds, with R1 20/20:

| arm | paired difference | seeds positive |
|---|---|---|
| `local` | +0.0117 | 14/20 |
| `wave` | +0.0080 | 11/20 |
| **`rule`** | **+0.0512** | **18/20** |
| `ab` | +0.0172 | 15/20 |

Eighteen of twenty seeds in one direction is not "absent, or equal in all arms, or smaller than the difference between two implementations". It is a four-fold effect over the map arm, with a sign test that is significant on its own (binomial 18/20, p ≈ 0.0004). The manifesto's "four designs were built to make a signal pay. In every one, the effect was either absent…" is false against the record it claims to summarise, and the false sentence is load-bearing for Claim 2.

The document's appendix does not cite article 034, and the number gate does not check for it (Finding 8). The signal line's own reports are also inconsistent with the manifesto's framing: `reports/2026-11-07-s72-e8d-replication-failed.md` and `-s80-e11-retract-e10.md` describe a *replication* failure on 10 seeds, not an inert manipulation; `-s83-e12b-predictive-signal-works.md` then reports +0.0124 with 8/10 — which `lab/reviews/2026-11-07-033-independent.md` finding 10 shows is bit-identical to an *unpaired* artifact (`s80_lag10.json`), so the "paired design" claim was never implemented. So the four-signal story is a mess of a different kind than the manifesto says: the failures were power and pairing failures, and the success (034) is a different mechanism (the signal changes the cell's upkeep, i.e. it changes the *rule the cell obeys*).

That last point is the real problem. Article 034's positive result is **precisely** the case the manifesto says cannot exist: the signal is "a term in a rule" — the rule is ours — and yet the behaviour was not "we could have set it", because *which* rule the signal modulates was selected for across seeds. The manifesto's Claim 2 predicts 034 should not have happened.

**What would fix it.** Either (a) cite 034 and explain why a signal that pays is not a counterexample — that would require Claim 2 to be restated as something narrower, e.g. "the *set of rules* is ours, even when the *choice among them* is selected"; or (b) drop Claim 2. Option (a) is honest and interesting. Option (b) is what the evidence supports.

## Finding 4 — Claim 3 ("the graph cannot reach a different ontology") is asserted, not argued, and the literature exists

**What is claimed.** §3: "A question whose answer requires a **different ontology** — not a better parameter, but a different kind of object — is not reachable by extending the edge. The edge can only extend into the space the current ontology defines." And: "The literature we surveyed is explicit about this: the boundary is not 'we have not found the right parameters' but 'the class of models we build cannot generate a new class.'"

**Why it fails.**

1. **No citation.** "The literature we surveyed is explicit about this" is the only literature claim in the document and it names nothing. Per `AGENTS.md` §3f, a claim that "the literature says X" must come after a corpus search with DOI and reading status; there is no such citation here, no `library/` reference, and no board line. The corpus audit (`reports/2026-11-08-corpus-audit.md`) is about *our* artifacts, not the literature, so it does not support this sentence either.

2. **The weakest point is the word "ontology", which is undefined and does the entire work.** An ontology is a set of kinds. But "a different kind of object" is itself a *kind* in the old ontology's metalanguage — you can always describe the new kind as "objects with property Q". If the claim is that incremental extension cannot reach a different ontology, it is false by construction: adding a new kind of object *is* an incremental extension of the ontology. The document's own §3 says models "produced open-ended behaviour … by **adding a mechanism to an existing ontology**: a new instruction, a new resource, a new interaction. None produced a new ontology." But a new *kind* of entity is exactly what a new resource or a new interaction can be. The distinction between "new mechanism in old ontology" and "new ontology" is never operationalised, so the claim cannot be tested even in principle.

3. **The counterexamples are well known and the document does not engage with any of them.** Incremental extension producing genuinely new kinds is the normal history of science: thermodynamics' entropy was an incremental extension of mechanics and became a new kind of object; Darwin's selection was an extension of existing population thinking and produced a new ontology of lineages; in artificial life specifically, **open-endedness** work (e.g. Bedau's evolutionary activity statistics, Taylor et al. on open-ended evolution, the "novelty search" line of Lehman & Stanley) is precisely a literature about systems that generate new kinds by extending rules. In physics, **effective field theory** is the canonical case of a formalism whose ontology changes with scale by incremental extension. The manifesto claims a boundary and does not mention the field that studies that boundary.

4. **The claim is not the one the programme needs.** Even if true, "the graph cannot reach a different ontology" is a claim about *scientific publication*, not about *the world*. It would justify working outside the graph — which the document concedes costs "unrecognisability" — but it does not justify the four principles, and it does not bear on whether the living can be modelled.

**What would fix it.** Define "ontology" operationally: a set of kinds K such that no element of K is definable from the others. Then state the theorem you want: "for model class C, every extension of K by an element of C is definable from K" — and prove it or find a counterexample. Then cite the open-endedness literature and say why it does not already do this. As written, §3 is a paragraph of philosophy with no referent.

## Finding 5 — P1 is a methodological preference disguised as a falsifiable principle, and its falsification is not reachable

**What is claimed.** §4 P1: "A world is specified by **what is conserved, what flows, and what limits the flow**." Falsification: "A world built this way in which the behaviour is **identical** to the behaviour of the same world with the constraint replaced by a tuned parameter."

**What it is.** A **definition** of "world" plus a **methodological preference** for constraints over parameters. It is not a claim about the world.

**Why the falsification is not reachable.** "The behaviour is identical" is undefined. Behaviour is a trajectory in a high-dimensional state space; two worlds never produce *identical* behaviour unless they are the same world. The test as written can only ever return "not identical", i.e. P1 can never be falsified. To make it reachable you need a metric on behaviour space and a threshold — and the choice of metric is itself a knob, which is the thing P1 says it is removing.

Second problem: the test is **ill-posed**. "The same world with the constraint replaced by a tuned parameter" presupposes that a conservation law can be replaced by a parameter, which it generally cannot — conservation is a global relation, a parameter is a local number. The comparison is between two non-isomorphic models. If the behaviour differs, that is expected and proves nothing about the constraint's value.

Third problem, and the one that matters: **P1 is already violated by the programme's own world.** `reports/2026-11-08-three-levels-measured.md` and the whole `E09_pond` line run a resource field with `resource_max`, `freq_dep`, `inflow` — parameters, not conservation laws. If P1 were a real constraint, most of the 583 reports would be inadmissible. The document does not say this.

**What would fix it.** Replace the falsification with something a machine can evaluate: "In world W, the behaviour is invariant to a 10× change in every parameter of the flow term, and changes discontinuously when the conservation law is removed." That is a reachable test with a stated threshold. And acknowledge that P1 is a preference, not a theorem.

## Finding 6 — P3's "within-cell measure" is not what the document says it is; it is a between-cell statistic, and it is a knob

This is the most serious technical finding, because P3 is the one principle the programme is actually implementing (`lab/experiments/E18.json`).

**What is claimed.** §4 P3: "The primary measurement is **the fraction of a cell's behavioural variance explained by its current situation, versus the fraction explained by its own history.**" And: "A between-cell measure can be produced by a parameter (give one arm a bonus and its rank rises). A within-cell measure cannot: it compares a cell to its own situation, so no term we add can move it except by changing what the cell reads. **The measure is not available to us as a knob.**"

**Why it fails — four separate defects, all demonstrated.**

**(a) It is not a within-cell statistic.** `scripts/k_readouts.py:55-71` pools all rows from all cells into two flat lists and computes one eta-squared over the pool. There is no per-cell grouping, no cell identifier in the row schema (`acted`, `res_at_act`, `pred_at_act`, `energy_spent`), and no within-cell centring. It is a pooled between-observation statistic. The docstring's own claim ("measured inside the cell") is not implemented.

**(b) The document's own bonus example refutes it.** The document says "give one arm a bonus and its rank rises" is the failure mode of between-cell measures. I ran exactly that against `k_readouts.py`:

```
with cell bonus (0.3 for every other cell): situation_explained = 0.6549
without bonus:                               situation_explained = 0.9640
```

A between-cell bonus moves the "within-cell" measure by 0.31. The measure **is** available as a knob. The claim "no term we add can move it" is false, and I demonstrated it in one command.

**(c) The two fractions are not a decomposition and are confounded.** The document says "versus the fraction explained by its own history", implying the two partition the variance. They do not. They can sum to 1.65:

```
situation=0.9980  record=0.6555  sum=1.6536      (action depends only on situation; record is situation + noise)
situation=0.0053  record=0.9980                  (action depends only on record)
```

And in the first case the record "explains" 0.66 of the variance even though the action is a deterministic function of the situation alone. Because `pred` is by construction a smoothed function of `res` (the docstring says so: "`pred`, the exponential moving average of the resource"), the two explanatory variables are collinear by design. Any result of the form "situation explains more than record" is partly an artefact of how much noise the EMA has. The document's central claim — "the fraction of a cell's behavioural variance explained by its current situation, versus the fraction explained by its own history" — is not computable from this code as a comparison.

**(d) `eta_squared` with quantile bins is a flexible non-parametric fit, not "no model fitting".** The docstring says "a simple eta-squared, no model fitting, no assumption about the form of the relation." Ten quantile bins on 400 rows is a 10-parameter step function fit. It recovers U-shaped relations (I confirmed: a U-shaped relation gives eta² = 1.0), which is a strength, but it means the statistic has ~10 degrees of freedom and will overfit small samples. The self-test does not test this: all three self-test cases are monotone step functions, which is the easiest case.

**(e) The self-test is a tautology-by-construction.** Self-test 1 asserts that when `acted = 1 if res > 0.5`, `situation_explained > 0.9` and `record_explained < 0.1`. But `pred` is set to `(i*37 % 400)/400`, a scrambled sequence — so of course it explains nothing. The test proves the arithmetic computes eta², which was never in doubt. It does not test the property P3 depends on (that the measure is not movable by a bonus, that the two fractions are independent), and a test that cannot fail is not a test.

**What would fix it.** (1) Implement a genuine within-cell statistic: group by cell id, compute eta² within each cell, then aggregate — and log a cell id in `k_rows`. (2) Replace the two marginal eta² with a **partial** decomposition: fit `acted ~ res + pred` jointly and report the unique variance attributable to each (e.g. by nested model comparison or by orthogonalising `pred` against `res`). Then the two fractions do sum to ≤ 1 and the collinearity is handled. (3) Add a self-test that plants a cell-level bonus and asserts the measure **does not** move — that is the property P3 claims and the one the code fails. (4) Move the bin count out of the statistic or report the sensitivity of the result to it.

## Finding 7 — P4 is a true tautology, and its falsification is unreachable by construction

**What is claimed.** §4 P4: "An agent whose state is a record can compute only functions of that record. Therefore novelty cannot originate inside the agent; it must enter from the environment, another agent, or a source that is not a function of the record. This is a **constraint on the architecture**, not a preference." Falsification: "A cell whose entire state is a record, in a world with no external source of novelty, that nevertheless produces behaviour not predictable from its record."

**What it is.** A **tautology**. "Behaviour computable only from a record is computable from the record" is the definition of a deterministic function. The word "record" is doing no work: any state is a record of something.

**Why the falsification is unreachable.** The test requires "behaviour not predictable from its record". But "predictable" is relative to a predictor. If the predictor is the record itself, the claim is true by definition. If the predictor is a third party with more compute, the claim is false — a chaotic function of the record produces behaviour no *bounded* predictor can anticipate, and P4 would be refuted by any chaotic deterministic system. So P4 is either tautologically true (predictor = record) or trivially false (predictor = unbounded). There is no reading under which it is an empirical claim.

The document senses this: "We do not believe this is possible, and we state it as a claim we would be glad to be wrong about." But the reason it is not possible is not a fact about cells; it is that the test is not well-formed. A claim you would be glad to be wrong about, and which cannot be wrong, is not a falsifiable claim.

**What would fix it.** P4 is salvageable only as a **definition of the class of architectures under study**: "we will not consider architectures whose output is a deterministic function of a finite record, because we are interested in open-endedness and such architectures cannot be open-ended by definition." That is a **methodological preference** — a legitimate one, and honest. It is not a principle, it excludes nothing that could have been included, and it should be labelled as a scope restriction, not presented as "a deduction" that "excludes designs in advance" (which is what §4 claims makes it "more rigorous").

## Finding 8 — P2 is the only principle that is a real claim about the world; its falsification is reachable but the test is not designed

**What is claimed.** §4 P2: "The state that the agents modify must be **the same state that sets their own terms**." Falsification: "A two-way world in which the behaviour is the same as in the one-way world."

**Assessment.** This is the one principle that is a **claim about the world** — specifically, a claim that closure of the coupling matters. It is falsifiable in principle: build W1 (one-way: cells deplete a field that does not set their costs) and W2 (two-way: the same field sets their costs), and compare. Unlike P1, P3 and P4, the two models are isomorphic enough for the comparison to be well-posed.

But the falsification as stated has the same defect as P1: "the behaviour is the same" is undefined, and the two worlds differ in a way that guarantees *some* difference (a two-way coupling is a different dynamical system). You need a stated metric and a stated threshold before the run, or P2 will pass trivially.

**What would fix it.** Pre-register: "W2 and W1 will be compared on readout R; P2 is refuted if |ΔR| < δ for δ = [value], with [n] seeds." That is a real test. It is the only one of the four that can be made real.

## Finding 9 — The "fewer knobs" argument is invalid as stated, and the document's own §2 refutes it

**What is claimed.** §4, "Why these four and not others": "Each of the four **removes a degree of freedom from the modeller**… **This is the whole argument in one line: rigour here means having fewer knobs.** A design with fewer knobs produces results that are harder to produce and therefore worth more."

**Why it fails.** Removing degrees of freedom does two opposite things, and the argument only counts one:

- **It can make a result stronger** — if the removed freedom was the one being exploited to manufacture the effect. Correct.
- **It can make a model unfalsifiable** — if the removed freedom was the one that allowed the model to differ from its alternatives. A model with zero knobs is a single point; it cannot be wrong about anything because it makes no predictions that could fail.

The document asserts the first and does not mention the second, but §2 states the second explicitly: "**a system whose behaviour is a function of parameters we set can only exhibit behaviour we could have set.**" If that sentence is true, then a design with **fewer** knobs is **closer** to the class that "can only imitate", not further from it. Fewer knobs = fewer behaviours = a smaller reachable set = *less* capacity to surprise. The two sections of the document are in direct contradiction: §2 says parameter dependence is the defect; §4 says fewer parameters is the virtue. Both cannot be the argument.

There is also a category error: the four "knobs" removed are not the same kind of thing.

| principle | what is removed | is it a degree of freedom? |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | "the freedom to tune until the behaviour appears" | Yes — a real DOF |
| P2 | "the freedom to set the agents' terms independently" | Yes — a real DOF |
| P3 | "the freedom to move the metric with a bonus" | **No.** This is not a DOF of the model; it is a DOF of the *analysis*. Removing it does not change the model, it changes what you are allowed to report. |
| P4 | "the freedom to claim novelty inside a closed record" | **No.** This is a DOF of the *claim*, not of the model. |

So two of the four remove model DOFs and two remove reporting DOFs. The table presents them as commensurable and then sums them into "fewer knobs". That is not an argument; it is a list with a slogan attached.

**What would fix it.** Replace "fewer knobs" with the actual criterion: **a knob is bad if it can be set after seeing the data to produce the claimed effect; a knob is good if it lets the model be wrong.** Then state, per principle, which kind of knob it removes and how you know. P3 and P4 should be reclassified as reporting rules, not model constraints.

## Finding 10 — The evidence appendix overstates what the artifacts contain, and the number gate cannot detect it

**What is claimed.** Appendix: "the within-cell measure | `scripts/k_readouts.py` (self-test on synthetic data)"; "the four principles as constraints on a design | `lab/experiments/E18.json` (the first manifest written under them)".

**Why it fails.**

1. **`k_readouts.py` has never been run on real data.** There is no artifact in `morphogenesis/experiments/E09_pond/` containing `k_rows`; `grep` for `situation_explained` across `reports/` finds only `reports/2026-11-08-three-levels-measured.md`, and there the phrase appears only to say the report does *not* carry it. The appendix says "self-test on synthetic data", which is honest, but §4 P3 presents the measure as "the primary measurement" of the programme. A measure with no measurement is not a measurement.

2. **`E18.json` does not satisfy the very rules it is cited as exemplifying.** Its `sensitivity` field reads: `"expected_spread_of_the_readout": "unknown - this is the first measurement of situation_explained in this world"` and `"why_it_can_still_be_run": "the readout is a WITHIN-CELL statistic, not a between-arm difference, so it does not need the same power calculation as an arm comparison"`. That is a **waiver of the sensitivity calculation** under `AGENTS.md` §3c, and the waiver is justified by the false claim in Finding 6(a) — the readout is *not* within-cell. The gate `check_experiment_manifest.py` accepts this because it only checks that `sensitivity` is a non-empty dict (`if not sens:` — line 66). So the manifesto's flagship manifest passes the gate by containing a sentence, not an arithmetic.

3. **E18's manipulation check is a prediction, not a check.** `"how": "the arms differ in plastic_eta (0.0 vs 0.05) and the gate reads pred only when plastic_eta > 0, so the record arm and the no-record arm MUST diverge; checked by comparing the first 50 steps of both arms"`. "MUST diverge" is the same reasoning that failed in errors 1–7: it assumes the mechanism acts because the code says it should. The check is described in the future tense ("checked by comparing") and, as of this review, no artifact exists. The manifesto's Claim 1 says the fix is to establish *before* the measurement that the mechanism can act; E18 establishes it by assertion.

4. **The gate is a counter, not a check.** `scripts/check_article_037_numbers.py` verifies: 583 reports (a file count), 43 articles (a count), 45 rows (a count), 7 numbered rows in a table (a regex), 4 principles (a regex), 4 occurrences of a phrase (a string count), and that six paths exist. It does **not** check: that any of the seven errors is described correctly; that any claim follows from the evidence; that the four principles are falsifiable; that E18 passes the rules the manifesto cites it for; or that article 034 exists and contradicts Claim 2. A gate that counts "**What would falsify it.**" occurrences === 4 cannot distinguish four falsifiable principles from four sentences beginning with those words. This is the same failure mode the manifesto claims to have fixed.

5. **The corpus audit does not support the corpus claims.** `reports/2026-11-08-corpus-audit.md` is cited for "the corpus and its limits", but its §3, §4 and §5 are literally `*(filled after the table)*` — the interpretation sections are empty. Its table also reports "B. record only the ranking metric | **0**", which is impossible given the document's own premise that the programme's primary metric was the favourable share (a ranking metric). The audit's own category definition is broken, and the manifesto cites it as evidence.

**What would fix it.** The gate should test **content**, not shape: for each of the seven errors, assert that the named artifact exists and that the described failure is present in it (e.g. for error 2, assert the dose differs between arms in the artifact). For each principle, assert the falsification names a measurable quantity, a threshold, and a number of seeds — and fail if it does not. For Claim 2, assert that no artifact in the repository shows a signal arm beating its control at p < 0.01 — which would have caught article 034.

## Finding 11 — §5's stopping conditions are not reachable, so "a manifesto that cannot fail is a slogan" is self-describing

**What is claimed.** §5: "**If a world built under P1 and P2 produces behaviour that is also produced by a tuned-parameter version of the same world, P1 and P2 are empty**"; "**If the within-cell measure never disagrees with the between-cell measure, P3 is empty**"; "**If a cell violating P4 produces behaviour not predictable from its record, P4 is false**". And: "We will report these outcomes as results, not as failures. A manifesto that cannot fail is a slogan."

**Why it fails.** Each stopping condition contains an undefined comparand, so none of them can trigger:

- "behaviour that is also produced" — undefined metric, undefined tolerance. Given any two models, some readout will agree and some will not; the condition does not say which.
- "never disagrees" — "never" over an unbounded set of worlds. Unfalsifiable by exhaustion.
- "not predictable from its record" — undefined predictor (Finding 7).

The sentence "A manifesto that cannot fail is a slogan" is the strongest sentence in the document and it applies to the document. None of the three stopping conditions can be evaluated by a machine or by a reader. They are commitments in form and nothing in substance.

**What would fix it.** One stopping condition, fully specified: "If, on readout R, with n = [value] seeds, |R(W_constrained) − R(W_tuned)| < δ = [value] in fewer than [k] of n seeds, P1 is empty and we stop." That is a sentence a machine can evaluate. Three of those, one per principle, would make §5 real.

## Finding 13 — "Weaker assumptions are more rigorous" is a non sequitur

**What is claimed.** §4: "each of which is **weaker** than the assumptions the current designs make. Weaker assumptions are more rigorous, in the sense that matters: a result that survives a weaker assumption is a stronger result."

**Why it fails.** This is the standard Popperian/Lakatosian point, and it is true **only when the two assumptions are nested**: if A ⊨ B, a result that survives A is stronger than one that survives B. But P1–P4 are not weakenings of the assumptions the current designs make; they are **different** assumptions. P1 (constraints) is not weaker than "parameters" — it is *stronger* in the sense that it restricts the model class. P2 (two-way coupling) is not weaker than one-way — it is a restriction. P3 and P4 are restrictions on reporting and architecture. None of the four is a superset of the corresponding current assumption, so "weaker" is doing no work, and the inference to "more rigorous" is unsupported.

Also: "a result that survives a weaker assumption is a stronger result" is true of *assumptions*, but P1–P4 are *design choices*. A design choice that removes a knob does not make the result survive a weaker assumption; it makes the result *conditional on a different assumption*. Those are not the same thing, and the document conflates them throughout.

**What would fix it.** Show the nesting explicitly, or drop the word "weaker". If P2 is genuinely a weakening of the current assumption, exhibit the current assumption as a special case of P2's negation and show that P2's class strictly contains it. That would be a real argument.

## What would falsify the manifesto's own position

The manifesto asks for this and its own answer does not work (Finding 11). Here is one that does.

**F1 — Refutes Claim 1.** Take all 45 rows of `docs/eliminations.md`, classify each by an explicit rule fixed before classification into {inert manipulation, confounded comparison, measurement error, underpowered claim, mischaracterised mechanism, other}, and publish the distribution. If inert manipulation is not the modal class, or if the bit-identity check would catch fewer than half the rows, Claim 1's "common shape" is a narrative and must be withdrawn.

**F2 — Refutes Claim 2.** Exhibit one design in the current literature, implemented in this repository, in which a behaviour appears that was not an explicit term in any rule, and which survives removal of every term the experimenter set. Article 034 is a partial instance already (the `rule` arm, +0.0512, 18/20): the signal does not add a term, it *modulates the cell's upkeep*, and which cells benefit is decided by selection, not by the modeller. If 034 stands, Claim 2 as written is false. A stronger version of F2: find a published system where the *set of rules* is selected rather than the *values of rules* (e.g. a genetic-programming or rule-discovery system), and show that its behaviour was not "we could have set". If such a system exists — and it does — Claim 2 is refuted.

**F3 — Refutes Claim 3.** Produce a citation, from the corpus, of an incremental extension that produced a new kind of object, and show that the new kind is not definable from the old. Candidates to check first: effective field theory (ontology changes with scale), Bedau's evolutionary activity statistics, Taylor et al. on open-ended evolution, Lehman & Stanley's novelty search. If any of these is an incremental extension that produced a new ontology, Claim 3 is false.

**F4 — Refutes P3.** Run `k_readouts.py` on a world where the action is a deterministic function of the situation alone, and where a cell-level bonus is applied to half the cells. If `situation_explained` moves by more than 0.05, P3's central claim ("the measure is not available to us as a knob") is false. **I have already run this: it moves by 0.31.** P3 is refuted as implemented. The refutation stands unless the code is changed to a genuine within-cell statistic and the test is repeated.

**F5 — Refutes P4.** Specify a predictor. If the predictor is the record itself, P4 is a tautology and is not a principle. If the predictor is any bounded agent with more compute than the cell, then any chaotic deterministic function of the record refutes P4. Either way P4 is not an empirical claim, and no experiment can falsify it.

**F6 — Refutes the "fewer knobs" argument.** Find one case where removing a knob made a result *less* falsifiable — i.e. where the knob was the model's only route to differing from its alternative. The document's own §2 supplies the general form of such a case: fewer knobs = smaller reachable set = less capacity to surprise. A single worked example (e.g. a model with one parameter that can predict either of two outcomes, versus a model with zero parameters that predicts nothing) would show the argument is not sound.

**F7 — Refutes the whole document.** Show that the four principles, taken together, do not exclude the designs the document says they exclude. Specifically: build a world under P1–P4 that is a tuned-parameter model in disguise — every "constraint" is a parameter in a different notation, every "two-way coupling" is a term the modeller wrote, every "within-cell measure" is a pooled statistic. If such a world passes all four principles and still "only imitates", the principles do not do the work the manifesto assigns them. Given Findings 5–9, I believe this world is easy to build, and that is the single weakest point of the document.

## Single weakest point

**P3, as implemented and as claimed.** It is the only principle the programme is actually building on (`E18.json`), it is presented as the measure that "is not available to us as a knob", and it is false in three independent ways: it is not a within-cell statistic (pooled eta-squared over flat lists), it is movable by exactly the bonus the document says cannot move it (0.6549 vs 0.9640, demonstrated), and its two "fractions" are confounded so that they do not decompose the variance (sum 1.65, demonstrated). The self-test passes because it tests monotone step functions, which is the easy case. The manifesto's flagship manifest, E18, **waives** the sensitivity calculation by citing the false claim that the readout is within-cell, and the gate accepts the waiver because it only checks that a `sensitivity` key exists.

Everything else in the document is either a tautology (Claim 2, P4), a post-hoc narrative (Claim 1), an unsupported assertion with a counterexample in the same repository (Claim 3, article 034), an invalid argument (fewer knobs, weaker assumptions), or a stopping condition that cannot trigger. But P3 is the one where the document's own code, run in one command, refutes the document's own sentence. That is the point at which the manifesto stops being a description of a method and becomes a description of an intention.
